# Episode 10: Around the Gate

## Logline

A village girl walks up the drive and asks Elaya, out loud, for a specific thing
with a date on it. It takes eleven seconds. Nine episodes of working out what
people will not ask for, undone by somebody asking.

## The Wedding

The church roof is off for the summer and there is nowhere in the valley that
will hold sixty people and keep the rain out.

So **Katrin Vogel**, 22, of the dairy, walks up the drive in October and asks
whether she can be married in the hall, on the eleventh, and offers to pay what
she can.

Out loud. To Elaya's face. Naming the thing, with a date.

**This is the inversion the whole series has been building.** Elaya arranges the
wedding — the hall under the painted sky, the lift running, rooms for the aunts,
and forty places finally laid for forty people who arrive and eat off them.

And for the first time in her life the work is **commissioned, credited, thanked
for by name, and paid for.** She hates roughly a third of that and the audience
should be able to see which third.

**Emil Roth**, 24, the groom, farms above the treeline. He is one of the four
farms Tobin walks the post to on foot, and the only person in the valley who has
spoken to Tobin every week for six years. He is the one who mentions, as a fact
about a building, that the wedding is at the hotel — and watches Tobin receive
that and has no idea what he has just watched.

Tobin comes to the wedding. Not in the grey coat, not with a mailbag, standing in
a room full of people, for the first time in six hundred and twenty-four visits.

## The Other Guests

**The Brosz family.** Father, mother, two children. Three cases, a folding pram,
a football. They booked because the hotel was in a brochure.

There is no mystery. Elaya reads their post out of habit on the first night and
finds a gas bill and a postcard about a cousin's leg.

They stay four nights, eat enormously, use the pool, run in the corridors, break
one gold-rimmed plate and offer to pay for it, and leave a note at the desk
thanking Doro by name.

**The building sounds different with them in it.** That is the whole of their
function and it should be the warmest thing in the series.

## The Return

**Havel** comes back, briefly, from episode 1. He does not ask for a room. He has
come to say that his wife's name is Ilse and that she liked the shutter open a
third, and that he has never told anyone that, and that he did not need to.

Then he goes down the drive, stops at the gate — and walks **around** it.

## The Answer

Three objects, one answer: the tab with the number rubbed blank, the one bell
rewired to room eleven, and the gloves.

**The gloves hide nothing.** No scar, no burn, no mark, no story. There is
nothing under them and there never was.

Aunt Mareau wore them in the garden, in the dirt, in a photograph where nobody in
the frame reacts — because a woman with an unexplained thing about her is a woman
people leave alone, and being left alone was the entire project. She rubbed the
number off a tab so nobody could ring for that room. She moved the one working
bell onto the room where her own life had happened. She put on gloves.

Three versions of the same gesture: **make the private thing unaskable.**

Elaya inherited the habit at nine, before she inherited the reason, and has been
performing a mystery she does not have for sixteen years. That is why she can
read everyone and be read by no one. That is why she is good at this. That is
what episode 6 was actually about.

She says this to Tobin, badly, in about nine sentences, in the forecourt, in the
morning.

He says: *I know. I have the dates.*

## The Ending, Which Must Be Small

A hand. A threshold. **No swelling music.**

Whether the glove comes off is a staging decision and either version works, but
the promise behind it is spoken aloud, and that is the payoff, not the skin.

Last minute: room seventeen gets a bell fitted — twenty-three now. The youngest Brosz child walks around the gate because
he wants to, and nobody stops him.

## Unease

**None.** Episode 10 is the one that lets you breathe, and that is its effect.
