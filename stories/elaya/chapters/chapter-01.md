# Episode 1: The One Bell That Rings

## Logline

A guest asks for a room by number. Elaya gives him a different one, works out why
he wanted it, and puts him back where he meant to be while he sleeps.

## Intent

Establish the hotel's beauty and emptiness, Elaya's method, the gloves as an
unremarked fact, and the locked door she does not open. The unease is placed last
and never remarked on: she moves a sleeping man's belongings between rooms.

## Chapter Text

The bell board is fitted in 1911, for a hotel that expects forty people and gets
them, briefly. Twenty-two brass tabs in two rows in a case of gilt and mahogany,
each with a room number engraved beneath it in a copperplate hand that has
outlasted the man who cut it.

Twenty-one of the bells are disconnected in 1963, when the wiring is done properly
for the first and last time. The electrician keeps one live. Nobody asks him
which, and he has been dead eleven years.

Elaya polishes the board every Thursday, tab by tab, without hurrying. She has
done this since she was nine. She wears pink opera gloves to the elbow while she
does it, and while she does everything else.

The tab in the second row, seventh from the left, has no number. The engraving is
there — she can feel the grooves through the glove — but the brass above it has
been rubbed down to a soft blank, the way a coin goes smooth in a pocket. Somebody
did that on purpose, and did it patiently, over years.

Her aunt never mentioned it. Elaya has never asked. This is the house's way of
being a family: things are left where they are, and everyone agrees not to see
them.

---

The hotel is beautiful.

This should be said plainly, because it is the fact everything else in this story
rests on. Seven storeys of shell pink and butter cream above a formal garden. A
hall with a painted sky on the ceiling and swallows in it. A staircase in pink and
gold that two people use. A lift that works, with buttons from G to 8, that Elaya
has taken four times in her life, because the lift is for guests.

Every one of the twenty-two rooms that have bells is made up. Every surface in
them is polished. There are fresh flowers in the
urn by the desk, changed on Mondays, and in the dining room forty places are laid
each morning with gold-rimmed china, and each evening Doro clears thirty-seven of
them, unused, and washes them anyway.

Nobody comes.

---

The guest comes up the track on a Tuesday, in a good coat and the wrong shoes.

He is sixty-something and carries one bag, which is the first thing Elaya notices,
because people who come to Villa Mareau for a week bring three. He stops at the
meadow gate.

The gate is a pair of gilded iron doors, scrolled and finialled, standing open in
a field of wildflowers. There has been no wall on either side of it for forty
years. It gates nothing. A person could pass around it in three steps.

He walks through it. Everyone does.

In the hall he stands at the bottom of the staircase, which is built for a crowd,
and he is not a crowd. Elaya comes down at her own speed.

"You'll want four," she says. "It has the morning."

"I asked for eleven."

"You did."

She takes his bag. He does not let go of it immediately, and then he does, and
something in the way his fingers open tells her that the bag is not heavy and has
never been the point.

"Is there someone I should speak to," he says. "The owner."

"There isn't, really."

She is already climbing. Behind her he says his name is Havel, and she says
nothing at all, which is not rudeness. It is how she works. A name given at the
door is a thing a person wants you to have. What she wants is everything they have
not decided to hand over.

---

At six she takes the Register from the desk and carries it to the linen room,
where the light is bad and nobody goes.

The Register runs from 1911 in one continuous hand. It is one hand because her
aunt wrote it in the hand of the woman before her, and Elaya writes it in her
aunt's. She taught herself the forgery at eleven. She is better at it now than her
aunt ever was.

She goes back through it. 1972, 1969, 1966. Her gloved finger moves down columns
of the dead.

*1961. Room 11. Havel, K., and wife. Six nights.*

She sits on the edge of a shelf, because she has never in her life sat properly in
a chair, and she reads the entry four times, and she counts the years under her
breath — *four, five, six* — and stops when she notices she is doing it.

The bottom left drawer, under the sheets, is where she keeps the other things. She
does not open it tonight. She thinks about it.

---

The dining room seats forty. Doro lays it for three, as she does every night, and
Havel eats alone at the one table with chairs drawn in, and Elaya watches him from
the door for slightly too long.

Behind her, in the kitchen, a brass tab drops.

A bell rings. Thin, small, entirely clear.

Elaya stops counting.

The tab that has fallen is the one without a number, seventh from the left in the
second row, and it is swinging on its little hinge, and the bell above it is still
moving.

It rings again.

The arithmetic, offered here because Elaya is doing it too: that bell has been
rung four times in thirty years. Twice by mistake, when a shutter came loose in
the same wind that pulled the wire. Once by a child who wanted to see if it
worked. And once on a Tuesday in March, by a woman who was already dying and
wanted the window opened.

Elaya crosses the kitchen and puts her hand near the board without touching it.

It rings a third time, and stops.

---

There are one hundred and six rooms in this hotel and twenty-two of them have
bells. She goes through the twenty-two in order, which takes until one in the
morning.

The other eighty-four are locked. She has the keys. She does not go in them, and
has not since she was nine, and could not tell you why in a sentence that would
satisfy anybody.

She does not take the lift. She carries no light, because she knows the building
the way you know your own teeth, and she pulls the bell rope in each room and
listens for the kitchen. Room one: nothing. Room two: nothing. Three, five, six,
seven — the ropes hang soft and beautiful and connected to nothing at all, and the
hotel is so quiet she can hear the wire *not* moving.

Room nine's rope is perfect. All the ropes are perfect. She has them re-silked
every three years for a system that has not worked since before she was born.

Room ten is where she stops for a while, because room ten is next to eleven, and
eleven has a strip of warm light under the door.

She does not go in. She goes back down and sits on the arm of a chair in the hall
and works out what she already knows.

Havel is in eleven. Havel, who was put in four, and who has let himself into a
room he was not given, in the dark, and is sitting in it. And somewhere in that
room he has put his hand on a bell rope, perhaps without meaning to, perhaps just
to hold something, and the one bell in this hotel that still works is the one that
answers room eleven.

Her aunt rewired it. Her aunt, who scratched the number off the tab, and left the
board hanging where anyone could see it, and never once said why.

---

Here is what Elaya does not do.

She does not go up and knock. She does not ask him whether he stayed here before,
or when, or with whom, though she has the answer downstairs in a book in her own
forged handwriting. She does not offer him tea, which is what the village would
do, and which would require him to be a man who needs tea.

What she does is this.

She waits until three, when the light under his door goes out and he goes back
along the corridor to four, because a man of sixty does not sleep in a chair when
there is a bed two doors down. She listens to his door close. She gives it forty
minutes.

Then she lets herself into room four with the master key.

He is asleep on his back with one arm across his chest. She stands in the doorway
for a moment and looks at him, and this is the part she will not think about
afterwards.

She takes his bag. She takes his coat from the chair, and his watch from the
washstand, and the book he has left face down on the counterpane, and she carries
all of it two doors along to eleven and sets it out exactly as a person would set
it out themselves — bag inside the door, coat over the chair back, watch on the
marble, book face down at the same page.

She turns the bed down. She opens the shutter one third, which is how it was in
1961, because the Register records the weather and she has read it.

She does not leave a note. She has never once left a note.

At four in the morning she goes back to the kitchen and hangs the tab straight on
its hinge, and stands looking at the blank brass where the number should be.

---

In the morning Havel comes down and does not mention it.

He has woken in a different room from the one he fell asleep in, and he does not
mention it, and Elaya pours his coffee and does not mention it, and Doro salts the
eggs twice and hears everything with her good ear and says nothing, and the three
of them sit inside the fact for twenty minutes without one of them touching it.

He eats at a table laid for forty. He looks out at the meadow, where the gate
stands open in the wet grass with nothing on either side of it.

"That gate," he says. "It doesn't do anything."

"No."

"Why keep it?"

Elaya considers this with more care than the question was asked with.

"Nobody's ever taken it down," she says.

He nods slowly, as though she has said something else. Then he goes back up to
eleven, where his watch is on the marble and the shutter is a third open, and he
stays four more days.

He never asks. Not once. Because he is a guest at a hotel, and this is simply what
a hotel is.

That is the whole of the transaction, and it is the only kind Elaya can bear.

---

On the last day she stands at the top of the drive and watches him go down towards
the bus.

He reaches the gate. He could pass around it in three steps.

He walks through it.

And Elaya goes back into a hotel with a hundred and six rooms, twenty-two of them
perfect and the rest dark, and passes the door at the end of the second-floor corridor — shell pink, gilded, no
number, the one bell in the building that does not work — and she does not open it
today either.

She goes down to the kitchen. She takes off nothing.
