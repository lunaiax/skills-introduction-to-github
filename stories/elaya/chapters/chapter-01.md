# Episode 1: The One Bell That Rings

## Logline

A guest rings a bell nobody can place. Elaya spends the episode hunting the room it belongs to and, finding it, finds her aunt's things exactly as left.

## Intent

Establish the villa's scale, Elaya's method, and the gloves as an unremarked fact. Ends on the locked room she does not open.

## Outline

1. Cold open — an object, examined. The narrator names it before anyone speaks.
2. The errand presents itself.
3. Elaya observes, decides, and acts sideways.
4. The errand lands on someone who does not know its source.
5. Button — one shot through the meadow gate.

## Chapter Text

The bell board is fitted in 1911, for a house that expects forty people and gets
them, briefly. Twenty-two brass tabs in two rows, each with a room number engraved
beneath it in a copperplate hand that has outlasted the man who cut it.

Twenty-one of the bells are disconnected in 1963, when the wiring is done properly
for the first and last time. The electrician keeps one live. Nobody asks him which,
and he has been dead eleven years.

Elaya dusts the board every Thursday, tab by tab, without hurrying. She has done
this since she was nine. She wears pink opera gloves to the elbow while she does
it, and while she does everything else.

The tab in the second row, seventh from the left, has no number. The engraving is
there — she can feel the grooves through the glove — but the brass above it has
been rubbed down to a soft blank, the way a coin goes smooth in a pocket. Somebody
did that on purpose, and did it patiently, over years.

Her aunt never mentioned it. Elaya has never asked. This is the house's way of
being a family: things are left where they are, and everyone agrees not to see them.

---

The guest comes up the track on a Tuesday, in a good coat and the wrong shoes.

He is sixty-something and carries one bag, which is the first thing Elaya notices,
because people who come to Villa Mareau for a week bring three. He stops at the
meadow gate.

The gate is a pair of wrought-iron doors, rust-brown, scrolled at the top, standing
open in a field of wildflowers. There has been no wall on either side of it for
forty years. It gates nothing. A person could pass around it in three steps.

He walks through it. Everyone does.

In the entrance hall he stands at the bottom of the double staircase, which is
built for a crowd, and he is not a crowd. Elaya comes down at her own speed.

"You'll want four," she says. "It's the only one that's warm."

"I asked for eleven."

"You did."

She takes his bag. He does not let go of it immediately, and then he does, and
something in the way his fingers open tells her that the bag is not heavy and has
never been the point.

"Is there someone I should speak to," he says. "The owner."

"There isn't, really."

She is already climbing. Behind her, he says his name is Havel, and she says
nothing at all, which is not rudeness. It is how she works. A name given at the
door is a thing a person wants you to have. What she wants is everything they have
not decided to hand over.

---

The dining room seats forty. Elaya lays it for three.

She counts the cutlery under her breath as she goes — *four, five, six* — because
she counts when she is thinking, and she is thinking about room eleven. It faces
north. Its stove has not been lit since before the funeral. Nobody asks for eleven.
People ask for the big corner room with the mountain in the window, or they ask for
whatever is cheapest, and Havel asked for a cold north room by number, the way you
ask for a thing you already know.

Behind her, on the kitchen wall, a brass tab drops.

A bell rings. Thin, small, entirely clear.

Elaya stops counting.

She turns. The tab that has fallen is the one without a number, seventh from the
left in the second row, and it is swinging on its little hinge, and the bell above
it is still moving.

It rings again.

The narrator's arithmetic, offered here because Elaya is doing it too: that bell
has been rung four times in thirty years. Twice by mistake, when a shutter came
loose in the same wind that pulled the wire. Once by a child who wanted to see if
it worked. And once on a Tuesday in March, by a woman who was already dying and
wanted the window opened.

Elaya crosses the kitchen. She puts her hand near the board and does not touch it.

It rings a third time, and stops.

---

There are twenty-two rooms and she goes through them in order, which takes until
one in the morning.

She carries a candle, because the upper landings have been on the same fuse since
1963 and she has never had the money to change it, and she pulls the bell rope in
each room and listens for the kitchen. Room one: nothing. Room two: nothing. Three,
five, six, seven — the ropes hang stiff, the wires long since cut, and the house is
so quiet that she can hear the wire *not* moving.

Room nine's rope comes away in her hand. She loops it on the bedpost and makes a
note to mend it, which she will not do this year.

Room ten is where she stops for a while, because room ten is next to eleven, and
eleven has a strip of warm light under the door.

She does not go in. She goes back down and sits on the arm of a chair in the hall
— she has never in her life sat properly in a chair — and works out what she
already knows.

Havel is in eleven. Havel, who was put in four, which is warm, and who has taken
himself to a cold north room in the dark with the stove unlit. And somewhere in
that room he has put his hand on a bell rope, perhaps without meaning to, perhaps
just to hold something, and the one bell in this house that still works is the one
that answers room eleven.

Her aunt rewired it. Her aunt, who scratched the number off the tab, and left the
board hanging where anyone could see it, and never once said why.

---

Here is what Elaya does not do.

She does not go up and knock. She does not ask him whether he stayed here before,
or when, or with whom, though the answer is standing in the room with him and she
could have it in a sentence. She does not offer him tea, which is what the village
would do, and which would require him to be a man who needs tea.

What she does is this.

She waits until three, when the light under his door goes out and he goes back down
the corridor to four, because a man of sixty does not sleep in an unheated room in
October, however much he wants to. She listens to his door close.

Then she lights the stove in eleven. It takes forty minutes to draw. She sweeps
the grate, opens the north window for the smell of the flue and closes it again
before the cold sets, and she carries his bag from four and puts it inside the
door of eleven, exactly where a person would put down a bag they were carrying
themselves.

She turns the bed down. She does not leave a note. She has never once left a note.

At four in the morning she goes back to the kitchen and hangs the tab straight on
its hinge, and stands looking at the blank brass where the number should be.

---

In the morning Havel comes down and does not mention it.

He eats what is put in front of him at a table laid for three, at which he is the
only person, and he looks out at the meadow where the gate stands open in the wet
grass with nothing on either side of it.

"That gate," he says. "It doesn't do anything."

"No."

"Why keep it?"

Elaya considers this with more care than the question was asked with.

"Nobody's ever taken it down," she says.

He nods slowly, as though she has said something else. Then he goes back up to
eleven, where the stove is warm and his bag is by the door, and he stays four more
days, and he never once asks who did it, because he is a guest at a small hotel
and this is simply what a hotel is.

That is the whole of the transaction, and it is the only kind Elaya can bear.

---

On the last day, she stands at the top of the drive and watches him go down the
track towards the bus.

He reaches the gate. He could pass around it in three steps.

He walks through it.

And Elaya goes back into a house with twenty-two rooms and four of them heated,
and passes the door at the end of the second-floor corridor — the one she has not
opened since the funeral, the one whose bell is the only bell in the house that
does not work — and she does not open it today either.

She goes down to the kitchen. She takes off nothing.
