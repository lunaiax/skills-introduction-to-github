# The Small Errands of Elaya

## Synopsis

Elaya keeps a grand alpine palace hotel that almost nobody comes to. One hundred
and six rooms; twenty-two of them have bells and are polished daily; the other
eighty-four are locked and she has the keys. Forty places are laid in a dining
room where three chairs are ever drawn in. She inherited it from an aunt she has
not finished grieving, and she has told nobody in the valley that it is hers.

**Every episode, someone checks in with something wrong about them.** A woman who
never opens her suitcase. A man who burns his letters unread. A guest who reports
a theft that did not happen. Elaya solves each of them the only way she knows —
from objects, from the register, from what people did not think to hide — and then
intervenes, anonymously, at night, and is never once thanked.

She is a very good detective and a habitual trespasser, and those are the same
skill.

Across ten episodes she performs nine of these errands and withholds one — and the
one she withholds works out anyway, which breaks something in her. Meanwhile the
postmaster, a cold and difficult young man named Tobin, has been keeping a written
record of everything she has secretly done for six years, and has never told her,
and is in love with her.

It is a love story between two people who cannot love anyone without first making
a file on them.

## Format: the case of the week

Episode 1 is the template and the only one already written in prose.

**A-plot — the case.** New guests every episode. Something about them does not
add up. The audience gets the same clues Elaya does, in the same order, as
*objects* — a laundry pattern, a signature written twice, a bed that is never
slept in, a ring set down in the same place daily. The solution is always
available before it is stated.

**Rules the cases keep:**

- The guest never confesses. Nothing is ever explained by a character sitting
  down and telling Elaya the truth.
- Elaya's first theory is wrong at least three times in ten episodes, and the
  wrong theory is always the darker one.
- The intervention is anonymous and the guest leaves without knowing anything
  happened.
- **Solving is not the climax.** In every episode the case is cracked with a third
  of the running time left, and what remains is what she decides to do about it.
- Half the cases are crimes and half are not, and the series does not sort them
  into two piles.

**B-plot — the season.** Tobin, the drawer, the blank tab, the locked room, the
gloves. The B-plot advances by exactly one object per episode.

**Recurring cast:** Elaya, Tobin, Doro, Frau Oberst, Peter Lind.

**The marriage pattern.** Every even-numbered episode is a marriage case — a
bride, a honeymoon, a wife eleven months in, a wedding — and Elaya interferes in
every one of them. Odd-numbered episodes are the solitary cases. A woman who has
never been close to anybody spends half her working life adjusting other people's
marriages, and the series never once has a character remark on it.

## Tone

**Amelie, but the series knows what Amelie is actually describing.** A woman who
manipulates strangers for their own good, enters rooms she was not invited into,
and takes things that will not be missed. Charming from one angle and frightening
from another, and this series looks from both.

Each episode should be pleasant to sit inside and leave one thing under the skin.
Never horror, never a threat — the unease comes from behaviour, not danger.

## Rules the series keeps

- **The narrator names objects, never feelings.** "The bell was brass. It had been
  rung four times in thirty years." Never "she felt alone."
- **Every errand is anonymous.** Elaya is never thanked on screen until episode 9,
  when it is done *to* her instead.
- **The gloves are looked at every episode and explained in none before 10.**
- **One shot through the meadow gate per episode.** Episode 10 walks around it.
- **One unsettling beat per episode.** Placed late, never resolved that episode,
  never remarked on by any character. See the table below.
- **No swelling music at the end.** The endings are small on purpose.

## The unease, episode by episode

| Ep | The case | The beat that should sit wrong |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A man asks for a room by number | She moves a sleeping guest's belongings between rooms while he sleeps. |
| 2 | A suitcase that is never opened | A child sees her sewing at the bus shelter at 3am in a ball gown, and nobody believes him. |
| 3 | A letter burned unread every morning | She steams open sealed mail, reads it, reseals it perfectly — and keeps one. |
| 4 | A theft that did not happen | Tobin recites a detail about her he has no ordinary way of knowing. |
| 5 | A bed nobody sleeps in | She protects two guests by feeding the committee an innocent third. It works. |
| 6 | A woman who cries at the wrong time of day | She does nothing, and it happens anyway. The horror is that she is optional. |
| 7 | Two strangers who stand the same distance from people | She arranges a perfect evening for them and watches it through glass, in the rain. |
| 8 | Forty stolen objects and nine days to return them | The drawer, open on camera, counted. And a glove that is not hers. |
| 9 | A guest investigating an errand from 1961 | The ledger. Six years, fifty-one entries, her secret life in his hand. |
| 10 | No case at all | None. Episode 10 is the one that lets you breathe, and that is its effect. |

## Structure

Ten episodes. Episodic on the surface — one errand each — with a single throughline:
Elaya's own life, which she is the only person in the valley not attending to.

- **1-4 Establish.** The hotel, the method, the valley, and Tobin's wrongness.
  Episode 4 is the first case they solve together: she has the building, he has
  the valley.
- **5-6 Test.** Her powers peak in 5 and cost an innocent bystander everything.
  Then 6 removes the ground: she withholds an errand and the outcome arrives
  without her.
- **7-8 Cost.** What the method protects her from, and what is in the drawer.
- **9-10 Inversion and payoff.** A guest investigates *her*, and the village
  performs an errand *for* her in her own method — and it feels like being
  handled, not loved. Then Tobin asks. Then she answers.

## The love story

Tobin is thirty, good-looking, and cold — not shy. The ordinary social machinery is
simply missing in him, and because he is not plain the valley has decided this
means arrogance. He is not from here; he arrived at twenty-four and has never said
why.

At nine he saw a true thing, said so out loud, was called a liar, and watched an
innocent man take the blame. Since then: never accuse, never let it go
unrecorded. His love takes the form of six years of observation, written down,
never used, never mentioned. **He is Peter Lind, twenty years on.**

**The audience should be uneasy about him from episodes 3 to 7 and should have to
revise that in 9.** He is not a patient warm man; he is a young man with a file. What
redeems it is that she is the same — she reads other people's letters, keeps their
buttons in a drawer, and has never once thought it strange.

He wrote everything down and used none of it. She writes nothing down and uses all
of it. They are the only two people in the valley who cannot love anyone without
first making a record of them. That is the romance, and it should read as a diagnosis
before it reads as a happy ending.

## Format

Prose, not screenplay. Ten prose chapters, present tense, third limited on Elaya
with a narrator standing slightly outside her.

The narrator states facts about objects and withholds facts about feelings. The
reader does the feeling; the narrator does the inventory.
