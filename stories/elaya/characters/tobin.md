# Tobin

## Age and the problem with his face

**Thirty.** And — this is the difficulty — good-looking. Not in a way he has ever
used or appears to have noticed. Dark hair he cuts himself, badly, straight
brows, a jaw the village has opinions about, and grey eyes that hold contact
about two seconds longer than is comfortable because it does not occur to him
that there is a rule.

If he were plain, the valley would call him shy and leave him alone. Because he
is not, they have decided he is arrogant, or hiding something, or both. Women in
the village have tried, pleasantly and then less pleasantly, and he has answered
every one of them with a fact and a silence. Frau Oberst has a theory about him
and airs it.

He is aware of all of this and has never once tried to correct it.

## Appearance

Built like someone who carries things up hills, which he does — the post goes to
four farms above the treeline on foot. The same grey wool coat in every season,
removed indoors with a formality that would be funny if he ever acknowledged it.
Ink on the side of the right middle finger, permanently. Very still. Does not
gesture when he speaks.

## Temperament

Cold. Not cruel, not shy — **cold**, in the specific sense that the ordinary
social machinery is simply absent. He does not smile to be pleasant. He does not
fill silences. He answers questions with facts and then stops talking, and the
stopping is total.

Children are slightly afraid of him for no reason they can name. Peter Lind is
the exception, and that is not an accident.

He sorts the week's post backwards — Friday first, Monday last — because "the end
of the week is heavier and you want it done while you've got the arms for it." He
has explained this to two people in six years.

## Backstory — the boy who was not believed

He is not from this valley. He arrived at twenty-four, took the post office
because the previous postmaster died in it, and told nobody why a young man with
that face would bury himself in a place of two hundred people. Nobody has asked
directly. Frau Oberst has asked indirectly nine times.

**At nine years old, in another valley, he saw a man take money from the church
box.** He said so, out loud, to adults, the way a child does when he still
believes that saying a true thing is the end of the matter.

He was called a liar. Loudly, and by people whose good opinion was the whole of
his world. And the man he had named let the blame settle on his own younger
brother instead, and the brother was sent away to work, and Tobin never saw him
again and does not know whether he is alive.

Two lessons came out of that, and he has never examined either of them since:

1. **Never accuse.** Speaking does not fix anything; it selects a victim.
2. **But never let it go unrecorded.** If nobody is going to believe it, it still
   happened, and it should exist somewhere in writing.

He started a notebook at nine. He has never stopped keeping one. There are
twenty-one of them in a box under the post office counter and only one of them is
about Elaya.

## What He Is Actually Doing

**He has been watching Elaya for six years, and keeping a record.**

Since the month he arrived. The first entry is dated eleven days after he took
the office: *a woman in gloves collects nothing, posts nothing, and reads the
notice board for four minutes.* By the ninth entry he had worked out what she
was.

The ledger is in the back room, in his hand, and lists every anonymous thing she
has done in this valley — the shelter repainted, the debt settled, the dress
returned, the evening arranged. Dates, weather, and what the person it landed on
did afterwards. **Fifty-one entries.**

He has never used it. He has never mentioned it. He is not blackmailing her and
never will. He simply could not bear for it to go unrecorded that someone was
doing this and nobody knew — because he is the boy who was not believed, and she
is a true thing nobody has said out loud.

**The series must let this be genuinely unsettling before it is allowed to be
moving.** A cold thirty-year-old man who has observed a woman for six years and
written it down is not romantic on its face. The audience should be uneasy about
him from episode 3 to episode 7 and should have to revise it in 9.

## Why It Works Anyway

Because she is the same, and worse.

She reads other people's sealed letters. She keeps their objects in a drawer with
labels. She forges a dead woman's handwriting daily. He wrote things down and used
none of it; she writes nothing down and uses all of it.

They are the only two people in the valley who cannot love anyone without first
making a record of them. That is the romance, and it should read as a diagnosis
before it reads as a happy ending.

## Why he is at the hotel — twice a week, for six years

**Villa Mareau is a sub-post office, and has been since 1911.**

When the hotel filled forty rooms a week, the village office could not handle the
volume, so the Post granted the villa its own mailbag and its own poste restante,
with a numbered receiving tray in the hall and a franking stamp kept in the desk
drawer. Guests' letters were weighed and stamped on the premises.

**Nobody ever cancelled the arrangement.** It cannot be cancelled by either of
them — it is held at the district office, in a file, by people who have never been
here. So twice a week, Tuesday and Friday, at twenty past nine, the postmaster of
this valley walks up a mile of drive to deliver and collect a mailbag for a
building with two permanent residents.

He has done this a hundred and four times a year for six years. **Six hundred and
twenty-four visits.** Neither of them has ever once suggested changing it.

## What that visit actually looks like

It is a fixed ritual and the series should shoot it identically every episode
until it changes:

- He comes up on foot, through the meadow gate, never around it.
- He does not knock. The hall door is never locked; nothing in this valley is.
- He puts the incoming bag on the tray. He takes the outgoing bag. He weighs and
  franks whatever the guests have left, at the desk, standing.
- **He is offered coffee every time and has accepted twice in six years.** Both
  times are dated in the ledger.
- He signs the receiving book. She countersigns. Their two hands, side by side,
  twice a week, six years — and hers is a forgery of a dead woman's, and he knows
  it, and has never said.
- Fourteen to nineteen minutes, door to door.

**This is where the whole romance happens.** Not in scenes about them. In a
recurring administrative appointment neither of them will end.

## Why it makes him a detective

Every letter a guest sends leaves the building through his hands, in the building,
in front of her. Every letter a guest receives arrives the same way. He sees the
addresses, the weights, the registered slips, the poste restante that guests
collect in the village so it will not be seen at the hotel.

He never volunteers any of it.

## Function in the cases

From episode 3 onward he is the second half of the method. **Elaya has the
building; Tobin has the valley.** She knows what a guest brought with them; he
knows what they posted, what came for them, what was registered, and who signed
for it.

Which means the hotel's own mailbag is the single richest source of evidence in
the series, and it walks in twice a week on legs, and says almost nothing.

He never volunteers any of it. He answers exactly the question asked, which means
she has to know what to ask, which is the only game either of them has ever
enjoyed playing with another person.

## The rhyme

**Tobin is Peter Lind, twenty years on.** A child saw a true thing at night and
told the truth and was laughed at.

In episode 2 Tobin is the only adult in the valley who does not laugh. He does
not defend the boy either — he writes down what the boy said, with the date, and
puts it in a drawer. In episode 9 that page is what makes the village act.
