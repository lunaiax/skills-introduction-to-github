# Art Direction — The Small Errands of Elaya

The locked visual bible. Every image generated for this series uses the STYLE
BLOCK below plus one LOCATION BLOCK, unchanged. Do not improvise new phrasing
per shot — drift across ten episodes is caused by rewriting the style each time.

---

## 1. The style, named

**Belle Époque storybook under permanent overcast.**

Three decisions define it, and they are all arguments with the reference:

**Period is 1890s in silhouette and nowhere else.** Elaya's leg-of-mutton
sleeves, high collar and elbow gloves are late-Victorian/Belle Époque cut. But
the palette is confectionery, the fabrics are matte cotton and taffeta rather
than wool and jet, and no other character dresses to period at all. The world is
*indeterminate mid-century alpine*; only she is dressed like an illustration.
That mismatch is a character fact, not a costume error, and the images must
preserve it.

**The architecture is Austrian spa, not French chateau.** The villa's yellow is
Schönbrunn yellow — the Habsburg imperial ochre on institutional buildings all
over Austria and South Tyrol. White pilasters, grey mansard roof, symmetrical
front. It reads as a *Kurhaus*: a cure-house built for a clientele that stopped
coming. Grand and municipal, never romantic or fairy-tale.

**Light is always overcast.** No golden hour, no god rays, no hard shadow, in
any episode. Alpine cloud, even diffusion, cool. This is the single hardest rule
and the one that makes the series look like itself.

### The Amélie argument

| | Amélie | This |
|---|---|---|
| Palette | saturated red and green | desaturated cream, blue, pink |
| Light | warm, hard, high-key | cool, diffuse, no shadow |
| Lens | wide, close, distorted | **wide, close, distorted** |
| Camera | whip pans, fast push | **whip pans, fast push** |
| Feel | delighted | fond, and a little sad |

Two rows now match on purpose. The camera grammar is Jeunet's outright — wide
lenses, close to faces, whip pans between compositions, fast push-ins — because
that kinetic hand is the most recognisable thing about the reference and there is
no reason to give it up.

What still separates the two is **palette and light**, and those now carry the
whole distinction alone. So they must be held absolutely: any drift warm, any
hard shadow, any saturation, and this stops being its own thing and becomes an
Amelie pastiche. The rule tightens rather than relaxes.

Same engine, same hand, opposite temperature. When a shot feels too charming,
cool it down; when it feels grim, the answer is never warmth, it is *symmetry*.

## 2. Palette (locked)

| Role | Colour | Hex | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| House / dress | Schönbrunn yellow, faded | `#EDDCAE` | Villa stucco, Elaya's gown |
| Ribbon | Powder blue | `#A9C2D6` | Elaya's pussy-bow, shutters in shade |
| Gloves | Dusty rose | `#E3AFAF` | The gloves, globe amaranth |
| Iron | Rust brown | `#7B4B33` | The meadow gate, hinges, the bell board's tabs |
| Meadow | Grey-green white | `#C6CCB2` | Wildflower field, wet grass |
| Mountain | Slate | `#6F7C83` | Peaks, distance, the flue |
| Sky | Flat overcast | `#DCDBD6` | Every exterior, every time |
| Interior dark | Warm near-black | `#231F1C` | Corridors, unlit rooms — never pure black |

Blacks are milky, not crushed. Highlights roll off rather than clipping. If an
image has a pure white or a pure black in it, it is off-model.

## 3. STYLE BLOCK

Append verbatim to every location prompt.

```text
Style: desaturated storybook realism, shot on Kodak Portra 400, fine grain, soft
overcast daylight with even diffusion and no hard shadows, milky blacks and
rolled-off highlights. Palette limited to faded Schönbrunn yellow, powder blue,
dusty rose, rust brown, grey-green meadow white and slate. Flat frontal
composition, symmetrical, wide-angle lens close to the subject with slight
barrel distortion at the frame edges. Deep focus.
Format: 16:9.
```

Notes on why each clause is there: *Portra 400* gives pastel highlights and
truthful skin without saturation; *overcast, even diffusion* enforces the light
rule; *flat frontal, symmetrical* enforces staging; *wide-angle close with slight
barrel distortion* is the Jeunet lens, and it is what a still frame can carry of a
kinetic camera; *deep focus* keeps the house legible, since the house's excessive
size is a running character point and shallow focus hides it.

Deep focus survives the change — Jeunet shoots deep too, so wide-and-close and
everything-sharp are not in conflict here.

## 4. Staging rules

- **Frontal and centred — and this survives the kinetic camera.** Doors, gates,
  corridors and facades are shot square on. The whip pan does not break symmetry;
  it *snaps from one centred composition to another*, which is exactly how Jeunet
  uses it. Composed like a picture book whose pages are turned hard.
- **Wide and close.** The lens is short and near the subject, with mild barrel
  distortion at the edges. Faces fill frame more than is comfortable.
- **Elaya is small in her own house — as a destination, not a default.** In villa
  interiors where she is alone, the wide composition holds her at less than a fifth
  of frame height and the room is the subject. The fast push-in then travels *to*
  her. Establish the emptiness, then close on the person in it; that move is now
  the series' basic sentence.
- **One shot through the meadow gate per episode.** Episode 10 is the exception,
  and the exception is the point.
- **Nothing is quaint.** No hanging baskets, no bunting, no chocolate-box
  shutters. The house is faded municipal grandeur, not a cottage.
- **The gloves are always visible** and never remarked on by the framing. Never
  a significant close-up of them before episode 10.

## 5. Fixed locations

Each block below is canon. Use it verbatim, then append the STYLE BLOCK.

### 5.1 Villa Mareau — exterior

```text
A three-storey 1890s Austrian spa villa in an alpine valley, pale faded
Schönbrunn-yellow stucco with white pilasters and window surrounds, a grey slate
mansard roof, symmetrical front elevation with a central double-door entrance
reached by a shallow stone stair. Twenty-two shuttered windows. The shutters are
thirty-one slightly different shades of the same yellow-cream, one repainted
each year. Wildflower meadow in the foreground, wooded slope and grey mountains
behind, flat overcast sky.
```

Canon: 22 rooms, 4 heated. The building must always look **too large for anyone
to be living in it**, and slightly institutional.

### 5.2 The Meadow Gate

```text
A pair of tall rust-brown wrought-iron gates, scrolled at the top, standing open
in a meadow of white and pink wildflowers. There is no wall or fence on either
side of them — the wall came down decades ago and the gates stand alone in open
grass. Grass has grown up through the bars. A worn footpath runs through the
opening, and the grass to either side is undisturbed.
```

Canon: the undisturbed grass either side is the whole story. **Everyone walks
through.** It must be visible that they need not.

### 5.3 Villa Mareau — entrance hall

```text
The interior hall of a faded 1890s spa hotel: a double staircase in dark waxed
wood sweeping up to a first-floor landing, a chequered stone floor in cream and
grey, tall shuttered windows admitting flat grey daylight, a reception desk with
a brass counter-bell, and far too much empty floor. Paint peeling gently high on
the walls. No people.
```

### 5.4 Villa Mareau — kitchen (the bell board)

```text
A large stone-floored hotel kitchen with a long scrubbed deal table, copper pans
on hooks, and a cold range. High on the wall, a servants' BELL BOARD from 1911:
twenty-two small brass tabs in two rows in a glazed dark-wood case, each with a
room number engraved in copperplate beneath it. One tab in the second row,
seventh from the left, has had its number rubbed smooth and blank. Flat grey
daylight from a single high window.
```

Canon: 22 tabs, two rows, the blank one is **second row, seventh from left**.
This is the series' most-repeated object — it must be identical every time.

### 5.5 Villa Mareau — dining room

```text
A hotel dining room built for forty, with tall shuttered windows down one side,
a moulded ceiling, and a dozen round tables under dust sheets. One table near
the window is laid for three, with white linen and heavy hotel silver. Every
other table is covered. Flat grey daylight.
```

Canon: laid for three, always. The dust sheets are the emotional content.

### 5.6 Room Eleven

```text
A small cold north-facing hotel bedroom: iron bedstead with a white counterpane,
a marble-topped washstand, one hard chair, a small closed stove with an unlit
grate, and a bell rope beside the bed. One window with the shutter half closed,
looking onto grey rock rather than a view. Bare boards. Nothing decorative.
```

Canon: north-facing, no view, stove unlit until Elaya lights it in episode 1.

### 5.7 Aunt Mareau's room

```text
A closed door at the end of a long upper corridor, panelled and painted
off-white, with a brass handle and no number. The corridor runs away from camera
with shuttered windows down one side and a worn carpet runner.
```

Canon: **shot as a closed door only, until episode 8.** The interior is not
designed, described or rendered before then, and when it is, it must be
disappointingly ordinary.

### 5.8 The Post Office

```text
A small alpine village post office: a wooden counter with a brass grille, a wall
of numbered pigeonholes behind it, a scale, string, and a franking stamp. Racks
of sorted letters. A man's grey wool coat hangs on a peg by the door. Flat grey
daylight through a small window with frosted lower panes.
```

Canon: the pigeonholes are sorted **Friday to Monday, left to right** — backwards.

### 5.9 The Bus Shelter

```text
A small concrete-and-timber bus shelter at the edge of an alpine village, with a
bench, a tin timetable board, and a single-pitch roof. Freshly painted in a
yellow that is very slightly too green, next to an older building in the correct
faded Schönbrunn yellow, so the mismatch is visible. Wet road, verge of
wildflowers, mountains behind.
```

Canon: episode 2 is the mismatch and its correction. The wrong yellow must be
*almost* right — the joke dies if it is obviously different.

## 5A. Motion vocabulary

For the video and animatic pass. Stills cannot contain a camera move — a still
carries the *lens* (wide, close, distorted) and the *framing at the end of the
move*, never motion blur. Do not prompt "whip pan" into a still image; prompt the
composition it lands on.

The moves, in the order they earn their place:

- **Whip pan between centred compositions.** The workhorse. Object to face, face
  to object. Always lands square on. Never lands on an off-centre frame.
- **Fast push-in on a held wide.** The empty room, then hard in to Elaya. This is
  the series' signature move and should appear at least once an episode.
- **Snap-back.** Push in, cut back out to the same wide, nothing changed. Elaya's
  cowardice rendered as camera grammar. Reserve it for episodes 6 and 7.
- **Insert whip.** Narrator names an object; the camera snaps to it and holds one
  beat too long. This is how the bell board, the blank tab and the gloves get
  their coverage without dialogue.

What the camera does *not* do: handheld, drift, dolly for its own sake, or slow
motion. Every move is fast, motivated, and ends locked off. Between moves the
frame is completely still — the stillness is what makes the moves land.

**Episode 10 exception.** The final sequence gives up the whip pan entirely and
plays in long static wides. Somebody walks around the gate, and the camera does
not chase them.

## 6. Continuity checklist for any new image

- Overcast. No sun, no shadow, no golden hour. **This rule now carries twice the
  weight it did, since the camera no longer distinguishes us from the reference.**
- Frontal and symmetrical unless there is a reason.
- Wide lens, close in, mild barrel distortion at the edges.
- No motion blur in a still. The still is where a move *lands*.
- Palette inside the eight locked colours.
- Gloves on, unremarked.
- If the villa is in frame, it is too big for whoever is in it.
- If the gate is in frame, the grass either side is undisturbed.
